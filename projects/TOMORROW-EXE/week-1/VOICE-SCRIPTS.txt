day 1 — AI Gets Your Wallet

What happens when your AI gets a spending limit?

Payment companies are already building infrastructure for purchases made through AI agents.

Now imagine groceries ordered while you sleep, and a flight booked before the fare changes.

You set the budget. It decides which purchase deserves it.

Then it buys something you never would. The receipt says approved. By whom?

Would you give an AI a thousand-dollar limit?

day 1.2 — Your AI Went to Work

It is 2032. You are late for work. Nobody noticed.

Your AI joined the morning meeting, answered three messages, and repaired the spreadsheet you forgot about.

On the train, you read a summary of a conversation you never had.

Your manager says the presentation was excellent. You say thanks, then open the file for the first time.

At five, HR asks a new question: which hours were yours, and which belonged to the agent?

The work got done. But if the AI did your shift, who gets paid?

day 2 — The Last Password

What if the last password you remembered was already behind you?

Passkeys already replace passwords in supported services.

Imagine that same ease at your office, your hotel, and your front door.

Every entrance works, until one account stops recognizing you.

Convenience starts looking like infrastructure when losing access means losing your day.

When passwords disappear, who holds the key?

day 2.2 — The Internet Leaves Your Phone

It is 2031. Your phone is still at home.

Your glasses place directions on the pavement. The bus time floats above the stop.

At lunch, the menu translates before you finish reading. Technology has become almost invisible.

Then someone says hello. A name appears, followed by where you met and what you discussed.

A permission asks whether you want to remember every conversation. You notice there is no permission for everyone else.

You can finally put your phone down. Would you turn People Memory on?

day 3 — Robots Cheaper Than Cars

What happens if a household robot costs less than a car?

Humanoid robots have already been deployed in commercial logistics. A home is a much messier problem.

Imagine carrying groceries, moving laundry, and cleaning under the table as ordinary appliance features.

The sales pitch is saved time. The real test is whether you trust it around your family.

And who fixes it when a software update changes how it behaves?

Would you choose a robot or a second car?

day 3.2 — Rent a Robot

You do not own a robot. You rent one for two hours.

At nine, a van arrives. A machine steps out and waits for permission to enter.

You point at the sofa. It scans the room, protects the doorway, and moves the heavy end.

For the first hour, this feels like renting a tool. Then you ask it to stay for another job.

The robot cannot. Someone across town has already booked the next slot.

The van leaves. Your furniture moved, and physical labour started looking like cloud computing. Would you let it in?

day 4 — Every Child Gets an AI Teacher

What if your teacher remembered every mistake you made?

AI tutors already offer guided learning. Now imagine one that stays with you for ten years.

It knows which explanation finally worked, and which question you were too embarrassed to ask.

Lessons become personal. So does the record of your confusion, attention, and confidence.

When you change schools, does that history follow you? And who gets to read it?

An AI might help you learn. Who should own the story of how you learned?

day 4.2 — The Classroom of 2033

It is 2033. One classroom. Twenty-eight AI tutors.

One student learns fractions with a pizza. Another gets a football diagram. A third needs the problem read aloud.

The teacher stops repeating the explanation and starts noticing who has gone quiet.

A tutor recommends the easier course. The student asks whether that means they are not good enough.

The teacher closes the suggestion. Some decisions need more than a prediction.

If every child gets an AI tutor, what should it never decide for them?

day 5 — Your Thoughts Become Text

What if thinking a sentence was enough to type it?

Clinical research has translated brain signals into speech for people with paralysis.

That is not unrestricted mind-reading. It is a carefully trained communication system.

Now imagine that interface becoming an everyday way to write a message.

Typing gets easier. Keeping the data private becomes a different challenge.

If an interface reads the signal, who owns it?

day 5.2 — Your Brain Gets a Privacy Policy

It is 2038. Your insurance app asks for brain data.

Sharing is optional. So is the discount beside the button.

At work, a second app offers focus analytics. It says the team will only see averages.

You open the policy. The message you intended to send is only one part of the data being collected.

You can refuse. But refusing now has a cost attached to it.

The interface made communication easier. Would you trade neural privacy for a discount?

day 6 — What If Nobody Owned Cars?

What if owning a car became the unusual option?

Commercial driverless rides already exist. A city without private cars is still a what-if.

Imagine calling a vehicle, arriving, and leaving it to find its next passenger.

It charges between trips. Your garage has nothing parked inside.

You save the responsibility of ownership, but depend on whoever runs the fleet.

Would you rather own the car, or subscribe to the ride?

day 6.2 — The City Without Parking

It is 2032. Your city starts demolishing a parking garage.

The residents expect another tower. The plan shows homes, a clinic, and a small public square.

In this version of the future, shared autonomous rides need fewer places to wait.

Concrete disappears. A tree arrives. The old entrance becomes a path to a playground.

Then a neighbour asks who this new space is actually for. Better transport did not settle the housing debate.

If your city needed less parking, what would you put in its place?

day 7 — AI Dates For You

What if your AI went on the first date for you?

It knows your schedule, your boundaries, and the things you keep forgetting to ask.

Two agents compare expectations before their humans ever meet.

They find a time, a place, and an excellent match on paper.

Then you arrive and the conversation goes somewhere neither system predicted.

Would you let AI arrange the date, or does the awkward beginning matter?

day 7.2 — The Video Call You Can’t Trust

It is 2035. Your mother calls. Your phone says unverified.

Her face looks right. Her voice sounds right. She says she needs money immediately.

You almost tap send. Then you end the call and use the number you already know.

Your mother answers. She is fine. The first call was someone else wearing her face.

Watermarking and provenance tools exist today, but no universal badge can guarantee every caller is real.

In a world of convincing copies, would you trust appearance alone?
